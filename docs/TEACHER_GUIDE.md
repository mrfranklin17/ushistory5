# Teacher guide

## At a glance

- **Grade:** 5 (Washington: U.S. history from time immemorial to 1791, focused on revolution and constitution, 1763-1791)
- **Time:** about 5 class sessions of 40 minutes, plus one session to present reports
- **Grouping:** partners at one device works well. The game names two travelers, and the talk between partners is part of the learning.
- **Devices:** any browser, including Chromebooks. No logins, no installs.
- **What students produce:** 10 sets of research notes, 30 leveled reading responses, a timeline, a cause-and-effect match, and an opinion report they present.

## Suggested pacing

| Session | Stops | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Setup, Providence store, stops 1-2 | Taxes and representation; Patriots and Loyalists; boycotts |
| 2 | Stops 3-4 | Firsthand and secondhand accounts; the Declaration's argument; who was left out |
| 3 | Stops 5-6 | Turning points; the Haudenosaunee Confederacy divided |
| 4 | Stops 7-8 | Hardship and help at Valley Forge; allies and Black Americans at Yorktown |
| 5 | Stops 9-10, return to class | Compromise and the Constitution; the Bill of Rights; timeline and report |
| 6 | Presentations | Students present from the game's slide view (SL.5.4) |

The game saves automatically in the browser after every step, so students can close the tab at any time. To switch computers, or to hand in work so far, students choose **Get a progress code** (on the title screen, at every stop, and in the trail menu), copy it, and load it with **Load a progress code** on the other computer. On shared computers, each student login keeps its own save.

## Before you start

1. Open `teacher.html` (the Teacher Dashboard). It opens with a fictional **sample class** so you can see what the reports look like. Remove it with **Remove all students** when you are ready.
2. Decide on the trail mode. **Explorer** (default) is gentle and keeps the focus on the history. **Trailblazer** is closer to the classic game, so supplies and pacing matter more.
3. Preview the content warnings below and decide how you will frame them.

## Collecting results

1. When students finish, the results screen has **Copy progress code** (and **Download progress file** when the game is opened directly rather than embedded). Students can also get a code partway through, from any stop or the trail menu, if you want to check progress mid-unit.
2. Have students paste the code into a Google Form, a Classroom assignment, a shared doc or an email to you.
3. In the Teacher Dashboard, paste one or many codes (or upload the files) and click **Add results**.
4. Use **Copy summary CSV** or **Copy item-level CSV** to paste the data into a spreadsheet or gradebook.

The dashboard stores student work only in your browser. Clearing your browser data removes it, so keep the original codes.

## Reading the dashboard

- **Class literacy growth** shows, for each reading and writing standard, how many students are at each level late in the trip, the class's average early and late levels, and how many students improved.
- **Students** shows each pair's early to late average for every standard (for example `2.0 → 3.5`) and a growth arrow.
- **Student detail** shows the level at every stop, each Field Report answer (grade 5, grade 4 step-back, grade 6 challenge), every Time Journal note, and the final report.
- **Teacher level** menus let you override the game's estimate for any journal note or report. Your levels flow into the class summary and CSV.

### What the levels mean

| Level | Name | Field Report meaning | Journal meaning (W.5.8) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | Extending | Grade 5 item and grade 6 challenge correct | Meets grade 5, uses 2+ sources, and explains which source to trust (W.6.8) |
| 3 | Meeting | Grade 5 item correct on the first try | 20+ words, 2+ history terms, own words, at least 1 source |
| 2 | Approaching | Missed grade 5, got the grade 4 version | Some content, but missing one or more grade 5 checks |
| 1 | Beginning | Missed both | Very short, or no content words and no sources |

A single question is a small sample. Look for patterns across 4 or 5 attempts of the same standard, and pair the numbers with what you see in students' notes.

## Rubric for the class report

The game estimates a level from its checklist. Use this rubric for your own score.

| | 4 Extending | 3 Meeting | 2 Approaching | 1 Beginning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Claim** (W.5.1) | Clear, specific claim that fully answers the compelling question | Clear claim that answers the question | Claim is vague or only partly answers | No claim |
| **Evidence** (W.5.9, SSS1.5.2) | 3 notes from 3+ stops, each explained in a way that connects to the claim | 3 relevant notes, each explained | 1-2 notes, or explanations restate the note | No evidence |
| **Perspectives** (SSS4.5.1, H3.5.1) | Explains another point of view and why that person held it | States another point of view | Mentions another view without explaining it | Missing |
| **Why it matters** (H4.5.1) | Connects to a specific present-day right, choice or debate | Makes a general connection to today | Weak or unclear connection | Missing |
| **Sources** (W.5.8) | Sources listed and labeled as real or fictional | Sources listed | Few sources | None |
| **Presentation** (SL.5.4) | Logical order, clear voice, steady pace, uses details | Mostly clear and organized | Hard to follow in places | Not presented |

## Discussion prompts by stop

1. **Boston Tea Party.** Was it a protest or a crime? Can it be both? Phillis Wheatley asks who liberty is for. How would Sam answer her?
2. **First Continental Congress.** Galloway's plan lost by one colony's vote. What might have happened if it had passed? How did ordinary families like Hannah's make the boycott work?
3. **Lexington and Concord.** Why would Parker and Pitcairn each blame the other? How do historians decide what happened when witnesses disagree?
4. **Declaration of Independence.** List the Declaration's argument in steps. Who was not included in "all men are created equal" in 1776? How have people used those words since?
5. **Trenton.** Why did a small battle matter so much? What is a "summer soldier"?
6. **Saratoga.** Why did the Oneida and the Mohawk choose different sides? What did each nation hope to protect?
7. **Valley Forge.** Was Valley Forge a defeat or a victory? Why do historians rely on Oneida oral history for Polly Cooper's story?
8. **Yorktown.** Could the Americans have won without France? James Armistead helped win independence but waited years for his own freedom. What does that tell us?
9. **Constitutional Convention.** What is a compromise? Which compromise helped the country work, and which one protected slavery? Why did Mason refuse to sign?
10. **Bill of Rights.** Which First Amendment freedom matters most to you, and why? Washington called the government an "experiment." Is it still one?

## Content notes

The game treats these topics directly, at a 5th grade level. Consider previewing them with students:

- **Slavery.** Phillis Wheatley, Jem (fictional), Prince Estabrook, James Armistead, Richard Allen, Lord Dunmore's Proclamation and the Three-Fifths Compromise all appear. Enslaved people are described as people who were enslaved, not as property.
- **Native nations.** The Haudenosaunee Confederacy was split by the war. Oneida and Mohawk leaders explain their own nations' choices. The scene at Boston notes that some Tea Party participants disguised themselves as Mohawk people. The map can show approximate homelands of some Native nations; it is a starting point, not a complete or exact map. Washington's *Since Time Immemorial* curriculum is a good companion.
- **War.** Deaths are mentioned (Lexington, Valley Forge) without graphic detail.
- **Humor.** C.A.R.T. makes jokes about Wi-Fi, powdered wigs and dysentery. The history itself is never the joke.

## What is real and what is fictional

Every voice card is labeled. Exact quotations appear only in the **Exact words** box with a citation.

- **Real documents and exact quotes:** the Declaration of Independence, Abigail Adams's "Remember the Ladies" letter, Phillis Wheatley's 1774 letter to Samson Occom, Thomas Paine's *Common Sense* and *The American Crisis*, Franklin's 1787 closing speech, George Mason's objections, Cornwallis's surrender letter, Washington's first inaugural address, Senator Maclay's diary, the First Amendment, and Washington's December 1776 letter to his brother.
- **Real people, adapted or imagined words:** John Adams, Joseph Galloway, Captain Parker and Major Pitcairn (adapted from their 1775 statements), Tyonajanegen, Thayendanegea (Joseph Brant), Benjamin Franklin in Paris, Joseph Plumb Martin (adapted from his 1830 memoir), Polly Cooper (from Oneida oral history), von Steuben, James Armistead, Richard Allen.
- **Fictional characters based on history:** Sam Whitcomb, Margaret Lowell, Hannah Brewster, Jem, Elijah Cole, Johann Becker, Lieutenant Moreau, the modern historian, and the storekeepers.

A good extension: have students find the real document behind one of the adapted accounts and compare it with the game's version (`RI.5.6`, `RI.5.9`).

## Accommodations

- Turn on **Read aloud** (on by default) for students who benefit from hearing text. Reading standards here measure comprehension, so read-aloud supports access without changing what is assessed. Note it in your records if your school requires.
- Use **Explorer** mode, and partner a confident reader with a developing reader.
- Students can tap any underlined word for a definition. Looked-up words appear in their Time Journal.
- The timed foraging game has a **Quick forage** button.
- The whole game works with a keyboard.

## Editing the content

All history text and questions live in `js/content.js`. After editing, run:

```sh
node tools/check-content.js
node tools/alignment.js > docs/STANDARDS_ALIGNMENT.md
```

The first command catches broken standard codes, missing glossary words and duplicate answer choices. The second updates the alignment tables.
